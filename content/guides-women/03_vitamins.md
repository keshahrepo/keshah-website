# Guide Three — What vitamins actually matter (most are a waste of money)

*A KESHAH publication*

---

If some collagen pill could stop hair loss, no one would be bald.

That's the test. Apply it to every supplement bottle you've ever bought.

Hair supplements are the most overhyped, oversold category in the entire hair-loss industry. Women spend hundreds, sometimes thousands of dollars on bottles that do nothing — Nutrafol, Viviscal, biotin gummies, hair-skin-and-nails packs, collagen powders, fancy multivitamins with "proprietary blends." All of it.

I'm going to save you the money.

---

## What doesn't work (and why)

### Biotin

You've seen this in every hair gummy on Amazon. The marketing implies it's the active ingredient for hair growth. The reality is far less interesting.

**Biotin deficiency is extremely rare — approximately 1 in 60,000 people.** Unless you have a confirmed deficiency (via blood test), supplementing biotin does nothing for your hair. Your body gets plenty from eggs, nuts, and whole grains.

Worse: high-dose biotin supplements can interfere with thyroid and hormone blood tests, giving you false readings. If you're trying to figure out what's wrong with your hair, you don't want falsely normal thyroid results.

**Verdict: Don't waste your money. Skip it.**

### Collagen

Collagen powders and collagen pills are sold with claims about hair, skin, and nail health. Here's what actually happens:

When you eat collagen, your digestive system breaks it down into amino acids. Those amino acids go wherever your body needs them most — usually muscle repair, gut lining, organ maintenance. **You don't get to direct them to your hair.**

There's no evidence that oral collagen supplementation stops hair loss or promotes regrowth. The studies cited by collagen brands typically show benefits to skin elasticity, not hair density.

**Verdict: Skip it.**

### Hair growth gummies / multivitamins

Sugarbear. Olly. Hum. Nature's Bounty hair-skin-nails.

These are mostly biotin (which doesn't help) plus a generic multivitamin. The "proprietary blend" language is marketing — there's no proprietary anything in a $25 gummy bottle.

If you genuinely want a multivitamin, buy a $10 generic one. You're not getting anything special from the hair-marketed version.

**Verdict: Skip it.**

### Nutrafol

You've seen the ads. The website's a masterclass in marketing. The price is $80–90/month.

Here's the honest read: Nutrafol contains a mix of saw palmetto, ashwagandha, marine collagen, and other "wellness" ingredients. Some of them have modest research backing for general health. None of them have strong evidence for stopping pattern hair loss specifically.

In the Reddit threads where women discuss what worked, the pattern is consistent: a few women report some improvement, but most say "I took it for 6 months to a year and saw nothing." For an $80/month bottle, "I saw nothing" is a brutal result.

**Verdict: Save your money. The ingredients are fine but not worth that price tag.**

## What might actually help (if you're deficient)

These three are different from the above. They have actual evidence behind them — but only if you're genuinely deficient. The rule is: **get tested first, supplement to fix the deficiency, then stop.**

### Vitamin D

Vitamin D plays a real role in hair follicle cycling. Deficiency is linked to hair shedding — not as a root cause, but as a contributing factor.

And here's why this matters: **vitamin D deficiency is incredibly common in women.** Especially if you live above 35° latitude, work indoors, or wear sunscreen daily.

**What to do:**
- Get your 25-hydroxy vitamin D level tested at your annual physical.
- Optimal range: **40–60 ng/mL** (not just "above 30," which is the deficiency cutoff).
- If you're below 30: supplement 2,000–5,000 IU daily.
- If you're 30–40: supplement 1,000–2,000 IU daily.
- Retest in 3 months.

This costs maybe $15/month and can have real impact if you're low.

### Iron / Ferritin

This is the most important one for women. Iron deficiency is a confirmed cause of hair loss, particularly in women of menstruating age, vegetarians, vegans, and women with heavy periods.

But here's the catch: **most doctors only test iron, not ferritin.**

Ferritin measures your iron *stores* — how much iron your body has banked. You can have "normal" iron levels and still be functionally deficient if your ferritin is depleted. And the level your doctor calls "normal" is too low for healthy hair growth.

**What to do:**
- Get your ferritin tested specifically (not just iron or hemoglobin).
- Optimal for hair: **70+ ng/mL**.
- "Normal" labs often say >15 ng/mL is fine. That's the threshold for anemia, not the threshold for healthy hair growth.
- If you're below 50: supplement iron (consult your doctor on dose — usually 18–65mg elemental iron daily).
- Pair with vitamin C to improve absorption. Don't take with calcium or coffee, which block absorption.

This is the single most under-diagnosed cause of women's hair loss. If you take one thing from this guide, get your ferritin tested.

### Zinc

Zinc plays a role in hair tissue growth and repair. Deficiency can contribute to hair loss.

But — and this is important — over-supplementing zinc *causes* hair loss (because it blocks copper absorption, and copper deficiency also causes hair loss).

**What to do:**
- Get zinc tested if you suspect deficiency (vegetarians, vegans, GI issues).
- If low: supplement 15–30 mg daily. Don't take more.
- Don't supplement zinc "just in case." It can hurt more than help.

## The supplement rule

Get tested. Supplement deficiencies. Don't supplement things that are already normal.

If your vitamin D, iron, and zinc are all in optimal ranges, **no supplement will help your hair loss.** The issue isn't nutritional. It's mechanical (scalp tension). Stop buying pills. Do the protocol.

---

## A note on bloodwork

You don't need a fancy hair specialist. You can ask your primary care doctor at your annual physical to add:

- Ferritin (not just iron)
- 25-hydroxy vitamin D
- TSH, free T3, free T4 (for thyroid — also worth checking)
- Zinc (optional)

Total cost via insurance is usually $0–$30. Without insurance, $80–$150 at LabCorp or Quest directly.

If your doctor says these aren't necessary, push back. Say: "I'm experiencing hair loss and want to rule out common deficiencies." That gets it done. If they still refuse, you can order most of these directly through Quest or LabCorp's consumer portal without a doctor's order.

## The bottom line

If you have a vitamin bottle from Target, Nutrafol, Viviscal, or any "hair growth" brand:

1. Look at it.
2. Ask whether the same ingredients exist in a $10 multivitamin or $15 vitamin D bottle.
3. Stop buying the expensive version.

Save the $40–$90/month. Test for deficiencies. Fix the ones you have. Do the protocol.

That's the whole supplement strategy for hair.
